\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM compression aims to reduce inference costs and memory requirements while preserving the capabilities needed for deployment. These requirements vary across applications: software engineering agents rely on code generation \citep{jimenez2024swebench,yao2023react,schick2023toolformer,liu2024agentbench}, whereas document question-answering systems require reasoning over retrieved evidence \citep{yang2018hotpotqa,ho2020constructing,trivedi2022musique}. However, the selection and configuration of compression methods, including pruning \citep{lecun1989optimal,frantar2023sparsegpt,sun2023simple}, quantization \citep{dettmers2022llmint8,frantar2022gptq,lin2023awq}, and distillation \citep{hinton2015distilling,gu2023minillm,agarwal2023gkd}, remain largely empirical because comparable resource reductions can produce substantially different capability losses. Identifying configurations that satisfy workload-specific requirements therefore often entails repeated compression and evaluation.

Predictive compression laws seek to reduce this evaluation burden by estimating post-compression loss from model characteristics and intervention settings. Existing laws predict aggregate loss from sparsity, precision, training format, pre-pruning loss, or teacher loss \citep{frantar2023scaling,kumar2024scaling,panferov2025unified,zhang2024scaling,busbridge2025distillation}, task-stratified quantization laws join model size, bit width, group size and calibration-set size \citep{zhou2026task}, a pruning law predicts each task's post-pruning accuracy from its unpruned accuracy and the pruning ratio, with an exponent transferred to a new model and, optionally, one pruned measurement that resets the scale \citep{sengupta2025compression}, and complementary studies document heterogeneous compression effects for individual tasks and quantizers \citep{jaiswal2023compressing,liu2025quantization}. However, aggregate predictions do not resolve how individual capabilities change, a single-method law neither compares methods nor states how many measurements it costs to fit, and task-specific observations alone do not provide a systematic method for predicting and comparing these changes across compression approaches. A configuration that preserves overall performance may still degrade a required capability, and existing predictions offer limited guidance on which method and level will satisfy a workload.

In this paper, we systematically investigate the predictive structure, measurement requirements, and generalization of capability scaling-down laws for LLM compression. Through a common experimental framework, we examine how compression responses depend on capability, model state, and intervention configuration, and determine when compact relations improve prediction or reduce the measurements required. We further evaluate how the resulting estimates support compression selection under storage constraints, separating the value of cross-method comparisons from the additional value of detailed loss prediction.

Overall, our contributions are summarized below:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A systematic investigation of capability scaling-down laws.} A common framework identifies the response structures that pruning, quantization, and distillation produce across capabilities, model states, and compression configurations.
\item \textbf{Measurement-efficient predictive relations.} We develop and evaluate compact predictive relations; a compact pruning relation fitted on half the development configuration measurements predicts four new Pythia states within 0.020 nats per token of the regression fitted on the full budget, and a pre-registered replication on OLMo-2 reaches the same outcome.
\item \textbf{Compression selection and decision analysis.} We independently test the cross-method benefit of selection under storage constraints and separate the value that simple method priorities, empirical predictions, and parametric relations each provide.
\end{itemize}
