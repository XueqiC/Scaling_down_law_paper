\section{Compression Method Selection}
\label{sec:selection}

\rev{How precise must a loss prediction be to change a compression choice? We answer this under nominal
storage constraints by comparing, on the same candidates, selection with parametric predictions (the
source-conditioned relations of Section~\ref{sec:laws}), with empirical predictions (development
configuration medians), and with a fixed method priority that uses no numerical prediction. Given a reference model, a capability and a budget, the decision is
\begin{equation}
(m^{*},\boldsymbol\theta_m^{*})=\arg\min_{m,\;\boldsymbol\theta_m\in\mathcal F_m(B)}\;
\widehat L_{m,c}(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\theta_m),
\label{eq:selection}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal F_m(B)$ holds the settings of method $m$ whose nominal storage, counted in stored matrix parameters, falls within $B$. The final rule calls
the pruning and quantization relations of Section~\ref{sec:laws} on a seen state and their
source-free medians on a new one; for distillation it calls a regression on the student state for mathematics and pre-specified constants otherwise (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:locked_rule}); a workload needing several capabilities replaces $\widehat L_{m,c}$ by $\max_c[\widehat L_c(M)-L_c(M_0)]$.}

\rev{On four references that supplied it no outcome, the final rule captures most of the cross-method opportunity for question answering.} Each reference carries twelve candidates at seventeen storage budgets (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:s3-identity}). Opportunity is the loss of the best feasible quantization candidate minus that of the best feasible candidate of any method, and a policy's regret is the loss of its choice minus that best candidate. On the 64 cells at which both policies find a feasible candidate the rule selects the new student in 63, with regret 0.000 nats on three references and 0.027 on the fourth against 0.41 to 1.84 for quantization only, meeting the pre-specified criterion (Fig.~\ref{fig:rule_main}); on mathematics, code and the largest loss increase the opportunity is at most 0.047 nats and the rule matches quantization within 0.0015. On 384 fresh question-answering items scored after the round, the rule's choice has a lower loss than the quantization-only choice on three references and is level on the fourth, while a generation readout puts the same students ahead on exact match on three, behind on one and no better than their initial students. Termination and the evaluation target are separate factors: a supervised end marker restores termination and raises the Gemma student's exact match, while the Pythia disagreement between loss and exact match persists (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_eos}), so a selector has to state whether it optimises loss or exact match. \rev{The quantization-only regret splits into selection error inside quantization and cross-method opportunity: on Pythia 410M its 0.59 nats are 0.47 and 0.12, and the rule's choice is 0.09 nats better than the best quantization candidate.} 

A retrospective comparison of the three policies on the same candidates (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:s3-ablation}) shows how fine a prediction selection needs. On question answering the
fixed method priority, the development medians and the final rule reach the same regret, 0.014 nats on
the Pythia pair and 0.000 on the Gemma pair, against 0.58 and 1.12 for quantization only: the benefit
rests on a stable difference between methods rather than on fine loss margins. On mathematics the
medians beat the priority by 0.024 nats on the Gemma pair, the smaller benefit that
configuration-level estimates add. \rev{The medians are read from the development panel alone, so this benefit needs no measurement
of the target model.} The parametric relations improve on neither, so their contribution
lies in measurement efficiency (Section~\ref{sec:efficiency}) rather than in selection.
