\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM compression aims to reduce inference costs and memory requirements while preserving the capabilities needed for deployment. These requirements vary across applications: software engineering agents rely on code generation, whereas document question-answering systems require reasoning over retrieved evidence. However, choosing between different LLM compression methods, including pruning, quantization, and distillation, and configurations remains largely empirical because comparable resource reductions can yield substantially different capability profiles. Identifying configurations that satisfy workload-specific requirements therefore often entails repeated compression and evaluation.

Predictive compression laws seek to reduce this evaluation burden by estimating post-compression loss from model characteristics and intervention settings. Existing laws predict aggregate loss from sparsity, precision, training format, pre-pruning loss, or teacher loss \citep{frantar2023scaling,kumar2024scaling,panferov2025unified,zhang2024scaling,busbridge2025distillation}, while complementary studies document heterogeneous compression effects for individual tasks and quantizers \citep{jaiswal2023compressing,liu2025quantization,sengupta2025compression}. However, aggregate predictions do not resolve how individual capabilities change, and task-specific observations alone do not provide a systematic method for predicting and comparing these changes across compression approaches. Consequently, a configuration that preserves overall performance may still degrade a capability required by the target workload, while existing predictions offer limited guidance on which method and compression level will satisfy those requirements.

In this paper, we systematically investigate how model capabilities change under compression and develop predictive scaling-down laws to guide the selection of compression methods and configurations. We first establish a common framework for studying pruning, quantization, and distillation through capability-conditioned loss. Within this framework, we vary model size, training stage, compression settings, data availability, and training exposure to identify shared response patterns and their dependence on capability and evaluation distribution. Based on these observations, we develop simple predictive relations and evaluate their accuracy, measurement requirements, and generalization to unseen configurations and model states. To translate these predictions into compression decisions, we further formulate a principled selection method that compares methods and configurations under storage constraints, and validate its decisions through independent evaluations.

Overall, our contributions are summarized below:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A systematic framework and investigation.} We establish a common methodology for studying capability scaling-down laws and conduct a systematic investigation across pruning, quantization, and distillation, covering different model states, capabilities, and evaluation distributions.

\item \textbf{Predictive capability scaling-down laws.} We develop simple laws that predict capability losses from model states and compression settings, and evaluate their accuracy, measurement efficiency, and generalization through independent tests.

\item \textbf{A principled compression selection method.} We propose a method that selects compression methods and configurations using predicted capability losses under storage constraints, and validate its decisions against baselines in independent evaluations.
\end{itemize}
